History and name

Hnefatafl belongs to the tafl family: games with unequal sides, a central king and an attempt to escape a surrounding force.

What does the name mean?

Hnefi literally means “fist” and names the king piece; tafl means board or game. “King’s table” is a common interpretation, rather than a literal translation.

What survives?

Archaeological finds and medieval literature place tafl games across northern Europe. No complete Viking-age rulebook survives. Linnaeus’s 1732 account of Sámi tablut is an important later source; modern reconstructions differ.

Sten Helmfrid’s historical survey brings together literary evidence, finds and references.

A board is evidence, not a rulebook

National Museums Scotland describes an Inchmarnock stone with a hnefatafl grid, among 35 gaming boards found there. Objects tell us about play, but do not preserve a complete set of rules.

The game on this table

Copenhagen is a modern ruleset, outlined in 2012 by Aage Nielsen, Adam Bartley and Tim Millar. This app’s Copenhagen v2 is experimental: exit-fort detection is conservative, and dead positions are not automatically declared draws. Mini is a separate 5×5 game with different escape and capture rules.

Published Copenhagen rules · Modern community and rules development

Playing each side

In Copenhagen, attackers try to capture the king. Defenders try to reach a corner or secure an exit fort. These are practical heuristics, not solved strategies.

Attackers: close escape routes

  • Coordinate groups so that one piece covers the gap another leaves.
  • Keep guards near the corners. Before taking a defender, check whether the king gains an open route.
  • Reduce the king’s space before chasing isolated captures. Inspect every gap after the opponent moves.

Defenders: make room for the king

  • Clear a lane, then consider the attacker’s reply before sending the king down it.
  • Look for two separate escape threats. One open path is easier to block.
  • Trade defenders only when it creates useful space. A threatened piece does not oblige the opponent to capture it.
Is one side favoured?

Observed community results for standard Copenhagen lean towards defenders overall and are much closer among the strongest players. Balance depends on the variant and players; this does not predict results against this app’s bots.

The source uses score ratios from complete pairs of games with colours swapped, counting a draw as half a point. These are not win percentages or proof of perfect play. Source checked 21 October 2026.

Before you move

Check the king’s escape squares, captures available to either side, and the space your move leaves behind. Play both sides, then use the table’s move history to inspect the first gap you missed.

Tactics

Three constructed Copenhagen positions, checked against this app’s engine. They illustrate local captures, not historical games or forced winning plans.

Files a–k run left to right; ranks 1–11 run bottom to top. The throne is f6. Ravens attack; harts defend the crowned king. Unlisted squares are empty.

Close a sandwich

Attackers: c4, e7. Defender: d4. King: f6. Attackers to move.

Move and explanation

e7 → e4 captures d4 between c4 and e4. Ordinary pieces are captured when an opponent closes a trap. Moving between two enemies does not itself capture your piece.

Use a hostile corner

Attacker: c3. Defender: b1. King: f6. Attackers to move.

Move and explanation

c3 → c1 captures b1 against the empty corner a1. The corner can complete a capture. An ordinary board edge cannot. In Copenhagen the king must reach a corner to escape; an ordinary edge square is not enough.

Capture beside the throne

Attackers: e5, g5, h4. Defender: d8. King: f5. Attackers to move.

Move and explanation

h4 → f4 captures the king on f5. Attackers win. The empty throne f6 supplies the fourth side here. Away from the throne, the Copenhagen king needs attackers on all four adjacent squares; the ordinary two-sided capture is not enough.

Watch for shieldwalls too

On the edge, a row of two or more ordinary pieces can be captured together when it is bracketed at both ends and every piece has an enemy directly inward. A corner can bracket one end. The king is not captured by a shieldwall. See the diagrams in the published rules.

Study game-playing algorithms ↗
Overview

Game-playing algorithms

Use tafl to study how algorithms choose moves, learn from games, and respond to other players.

Search, learning, and opponent selection are separate choices. A policy can act directly or guide search. Self-play can train a value learner, a policy-gradient agent, or a search-based system.

Search
Minimax and MCTS examine possible continuations.
Model-free learning
DQN learns action values; PPO updates a policy. Neither needs a learned transition model.
Model-based learning
MuZero searches a learned model. Dreamer trains behaviour inside one.
Multi-agent training
Self-play and populations determine which opponents supply experience.

Tafl as an environment

The sides have different objectives and piece counts. Rewards are sparse; repetition depends on history. Legal-move masks and an exact simulator let us isolate learning errors from rule errors.

Mini provides a smaller test case. Copenhagen changes both scale and rules, and remains experimental. Results on one do not establish performance on the other.

Scope

Random, heuristic, and MCTS are playable. Other methods are study material and candidate experiments, not installed opponents. No method has established state-of-the-art performance on Hnef.

Random

Selects a legal move with equal probability.

A policy determines how an opponent chooses a move. With 20 legal moves, the random policy gives each a 1-in-20 chance, including an immediate win.

Uniform sampling

Baseline

Random play is a baseline for comparison. It neither evaluates positions nor learns from results.

The playing-table bot is seeded: the same seed and position reproduce its move. This example uses browser randomness.

Winning moves

A winning move has the same probability as any other legal move. The policy does not recognize its result.

Heuristic

Scores the position after each legal move and selects the highest score.

This is one-ply lookahead: one move by one player. The score uses fixed weights for material, king progress, escape lanes, and adjacent attackers. Ties are broken randomly.

Evaluation

Adjust the features to calculate a defender score. Some combinations may not correspond to a legal position.

Defender score+0.175Higher favours defenders.
Attackers use the negative.

0.35 × material + 0.25 × progress + 0.30 × lanes / 4 − 0.10 × attackers / 4

Material = defenders remaining / starting defenders − attackers remaining / starting attackers; the king is excluded. Escape means an edge in Mini and a corner in Copenhagen. Scores are clamped to −0.9…+0.9.

Limits

The policy does not search the opponent’s reply. A high-scoring move may expose the king to capture. The score is not a win probability.

Terminal positions use the result: +1 for a win, −1 for a loss, and 0 for a draw, from the mover’s perspective. The weights are untuned and do not change through play.

Interpreting the score

A score of +0.6 expresses a preference, not a 60% win rate. Its relationship to outcomes would need to be measured.

Minimax and alpha–beta

Choose a move assuming the opponent chooses their best reply.

Minimax alternates maximum and minimum values through a game tree. At a depth limit it uses an evaluation function. Alpha–beta pruning skips branches that cannot change the result; it preserves the minimax choice at the same depth and evaluation.

Hnef experiment

Compare one-ply evaluation with deeper search on the same tactical positions. Order captures first to test pruning efficiency. Keep repetition history in each node. Depth limits can still hide traps beyond the evaluated horizon.

Monte Carlo tree search

Estimates move values through simulated continuations.

MCTS builds a tree of positions. Hnef uses UCT to balance branches with good average results (exploitation) against less-visited branches (exploration).

Search iteration

The four stages of one simulation. This diagram does not run a search.

  1. SelectApply UCT
  2. ExpandAdd an untried move
  3. Roll outPlay a random continuation
  4. Back upUpdate visits and results

Select: follow branches using UCT. Opponent turns favour lower values for the root player.

Move selection

Hnef selects the most-visited root move, breaking ties by average result, then seeded randomness. A discovered immediate win takes priority.

The playing table uses 32 simulations per move and up to 24 rollout moves after expansion. Each decision starts a fresh tree. An unfinished rollout contributes 0 as an unknown-value estimate, not a draw.

Limited search and random rollouts can miss strong replies. This MCTS uses no heuristic or network and retains no learning between games.

Rollout cutoffs

A cutoff ends the simulation before the position is resolved. It does not change the game’s outcome.

Minimax comparison

Minimax searches for each player’s best replies, often to a fixed depth. MCTS allocates simulations selectively. Neither requires training. Minimax is not currently implemented in Hnef.

UCT paper by Kocsis and Szepesvári.

Policy/value networks

Predicts move preferences and position values using learned weights.

Training adjusts a neural network’s weights from examples. A policy/value architecture has two outputs: a policy over moves and a value for the position.

Policy output

Illustrative values; no trained model is running.

Does Move A’s 60% policy weight mean it wins 60% of games?

The value output estimates expected outcome for a specified player, on a −1 to +1 reward scale. It is not necessarily a calibrated win probability.

Training and inference

Inference uses a trained network with fixed weights. Reinforcement learning is a training method that uses actions and rewards; a neural network alone does not imply RL.

Hnef’s environment provides board features, legal-action masks, and action history. Models must account for repetition history and exclude illegal moves.

Position history

The board alone does not record earlier repetitions. The model or its search must use history to distinguish these states.

No trained network is available in the playing table. Historical TensorFlow weights are not integrated.

Value learning · not implemented

DQN and Rainbow

Learn the expected return of taking each action, Q(s, a).

DQN fits action values using stored transitions and a lagged target network. Acting can mix the best legal action with random exploration. Replay reuses past experience; changing opponents can make that experience stale.

One value update

Scalar example, not a network training run. Current Q = +0.2; discount γ = 0.95. Values use the player-to-move perspective.

Q ← Q + α(target − Q)

For this alternating zero-sum example, target = −γ max Q(opponent). Terminal targets use the reward only. A collection cutoff is not a terminal draw.

Rainbow

Rainbow combines double Q-learning, prioritized replay, dueling networks, multi-step targets, distributional returns, and noisy exploration. These address different sources of error; they are not six levels of strength. Hessel et al. (2017).

Hnef experiment

Start with masked DQN against a fixed opponent. Compare replay sizes and multi-step returns before changing the opponent pool. Apply masks to target selection as well as played moves. Retain history for repetition.

Mnih et al. · DQN (2015)

Policy gradients · not implemented

PPO

Update action probabilities using recent experience and an estimated advantage.

An actor chooses moves; a critic estimates return. Advantage measures whether an action did better than the baseline. PPO reuses a recent batch for several updates, with a clipped objective to limit incentives for large changes.

Clipped objective

One illustrative training sample. Ratio r = new probability / old probability; clipping ε = 0.2.

min(r × A, clip(r, 0.8, 1.2) × A)

Try both signs of advantage. Clipping changes the objective; it does not impose a hard bound on every probability update.

Hnef experiment

Compare masked PPO with DQN under equal environment-step budgets, then report training compute separately. Test fixed opponents before self-play. Use side-aware rewards and history-aware observations; an unconditioned board image omits repetition.

Schulman et al. · PPO (2017). An established baseline, not a claim of current best performance.

Search-guided learning · not implemented

AlphaZero and Gumbel search

Train a network from self-play search, using the exact game rules.

Reinforcement learning (RL) learns from actions and rewards. In self-play, the agent plays both sides, sometimes against earlier versions. Search can provide training targets for the policy.

Training cycle

AlphaZero-style cycle. Other RL methods can also use self-play.

  1. PlayNetwork guides search
  2. CollectPositions, visits, outcomes
  3. TrainUpdate policy and value
  4. EvaluateCompare checkpoints

Play: guide search with policy predictions and evaluate leaf positions with the value network.

Rewards

Hnef gives 0 during play, +1 for a win, −1 for a loss, and 0 for a draw. A game stopped at the collection limit remains unfinished.

The policy would learn from search visit distributions; the value would learn from completed outcomes, from the appropriate player’s perspective.

Gumbel AlphaZero

Gumbel search samples candidate actions without replacement and uses sequential halving at the root. It targets policy improvement when simulations are scarce; guarantees depend on correctly estimated action values. Test it against standard search at matched budgets. Danihelka et al. (2022).

Evaluation

Compare checkpoints against the baselines and earlier models as both attackers and defenders. Tafl is asymmetric. Training time and model size alone do not establish strength.

Future model notes will record the training setup, ruleset, rewards, history handling, search budget, and measured results.

Training limits

Training can be unstable or overfit to its opponents. Fixed baselines and earlier checkpoints help detect regressions.

Reference

AlphaZero describes the combination of self-play, neural networks, and tree search that informs this planned approach.

Model-based search · not implemented

MuZero and EfficientZero

Search with a learned transition model rather than an exact simulator.

MuZero encodes a position into a latent state. Its dynamics model predicts the next latent state and reward; a prediction network supplies policy and value. Search uses these predictions. The model need not reconstruct the board image.

AlphaZero
Exact transitions; learned policy and value.
MuZero
Learned transitions, rewards, policy, and value.
EfficientZero V2

This 2024 method studies data-efficient model-based search across discrete and continuous control. Its reported results use limited-data benchmarks, not tafl. The relevant question here is whether model learning improves results per collected game. Wang et al. (2024).

Hnef experiment

Hnef already has inexpensive exact transitions. A learned model adds approximation error, so compare it with exact-model search at matched compute. Measure reward, capture, and repetition errors over increasing rollout lengths. Real moves must still pass the engine’s legality checks.

Schrittwieser et al. · MuZero (2019; Nature 2020)

World-model learning · not implemented

Dreamer

Train an actor and critic on trajectories imagined by a learned world model.

DreamerV3 learns representations, dynamics, rewards, and continuation predictions from replay. The actor and critic train on imagined trajectories. Unlike MuZero, the deployed actor need not run tree search for each move.

DreamerV3

The 2025 Nature paper reports a single configuration across more than 150 control tasks, including Minecraft. Those results concern its tested environments; they do not establish competitive strength in tafl. Hafner et al. (2025).

Dreamer 4

The September 2025 preprint studies a scalable video world model and behaviour learned inside it, including Minecraft from offline data. This differs from training solely through fresh environment interaction. Hafner, Yan & Lillicrap (2025).

Hnef experiment

Compare a compact board-state model with exact-engine trajectories before considering pixels. Hold the opponent fixed first: an imagined opponent must be modelled or supplied explicitly. Longer imagined sequences may amplify model errors that the policy learns to exploit.

Candidate study: vary imagination horizon and measure held-out transition error alongside playing strength. No world model is currently trained in Hnef.

Self-play and populations

The training opponent is part of the learning problem.

Playing only the latest checkpoint can forget earlier strategies or cycle between weaknesses. A league retains diverse opponents. Policy-space response oracles (PSRO) train approximate best responses to opponent mixtures and update the mixture using measured payoffs.

Current research

State of the art depends on the environment, data allowance, and compute budget.

PPO and Rainbow are foundations. Newer work studies data efficiency, learned simulators, stable multi-agent updates, and the discovery of learning rules. These are different research questions, not a single ranking.

Regularized policy optimization · 2026

Ota et al. study reverse-KL and entropy regularization in two-player zero-sum games, with experiments on five board games. The revised paper is marked accepted at ICML 2026. Its stability and efficiency claims apply to the stated theory and experiments. Paper, version 2.

Hnef question

Can regularization reduce cycling between attacker and defender strategies? Compare cross-play and training variability with PPO under the same sampling budget.

DiscoRL · 2025

DiscoRL meta-learns how to update policies and predictions from populations of agents across environments. The learned object is an update rule, not just one game’s policy. Its reported Atari and transfer results do not establish performance on Hnef. Oh et al., Nature.

Hnef question

Evaluate transfer of a learned update rule on held-out tafl tasks. Discovering a rule across many environments and training one Hnef agent have different compute requirements.

World models · 2024–2025

EfficientZero V2 examines limited-data search; DreamerV3 and Dreamer 4 examine learning through imagined trajectories. Exact Hnef transitions provide a reference for measuring model error.

Reading results

Record the ruleset, observations, opponent pool, collected transitions, training hardware, inference search budget, seeds, and uncertainty. A new paper’s benchmark lead is not evidence that its method is best for this game. This is a selected reading list, not an exhaustive leaderboard.
